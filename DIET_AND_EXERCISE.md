# OPTIMAL DIET AND EXERCISE
## An Evidence-Based Operating Routine for Long-Term Human Health (2026)

> **Bottom line:** The highest-confidence routine is not an extreme diet or a heroic workout plan. It is a repeatable Mediterranean/DASH-like food pattern, energy intake appropriate to the person, enough protein to preserve or build muscle, 150–300 minutes of aerobic activity per week, strength training at least twice weekly, and frequent ordinary movement. More can help, but the returns diminish and recovery matters.
>
> **Evidence current through August 23, 2026.** This report is for generally healthy adults. It is not a diagnosis, treatment plan, or substitute for a clinician or registered dietitian who knows the individual's medical history.

## 1. WHAT “OPTIMAL” MEANS

“Optimal” needs an objective. This guide optimizes for a broad health-span outcome:

- lower cardiovascular, metabolic, cancer, and frailty risk;
- preserved muscle, bone, aerobic capacity, balance, and independence;
- adequate nutrition without chronic energy excess or deficiency;
- a routine that can be sustained for years;
- low injury, burnout, deficiency, and disordered-eating risk.

It does **not** optimize for maximal bodybuilding, elite endurance performance, fastest possible weight loss, a biological-age test result, or a particular dietary identity.

### Evidence grades used here

| Grade | Meaning in this report |
|---|---|
| **HIGH** | Replicated randomized trials, systematic reviews of trials, or authoritative guidelines supported by a broad convergent evidence base |
| **MODERATE** | Strong prospective evidence and/or randomized effects on validated intermediate outcomes, but limited hard-outcome trials |
| **LOW** | Small, short, exploratory, mechanistic, or materially confounded evidence |
| **NOT ESTABLISHED** | Plausible or popular, but no reliable evidence of unique long-term health benefit |

Diet and exercise research has an unavoidable asymmetry: blood pressure, fitness, strength, and glucose can be randomized; decades of dietary or exercise exposure ending in death usually cannot. Accordingly, hard-outcome cohort evidence is labeled as observational even when it is large and consistent.

### The governing principles

1. **Pattern beats ingredient.** A healthy dietary pattern matters more than any “superfood.”
2. **Replacement matters.** Replacing butter with olive oil is different from replacing it with refined starch; replacing processed meat with beans is different from simply adding beans.
3. **Fitness has several dimensions.** Aerobic fitness does not replace strength; strength does not replace daily movement or balance.
4. **The first dose has the largest return.** Moving from none to some activity is more consequential than fine-tuning an already good program.
5. **Recovery is part of training.** A plan that chronically damages sleep, energy availability, joints, mood, or adherence is not optimal.
6. **Personal constraints are real.** Medical conditions, medications, disability, age, pregnancy, food access, culture, and preference change the implementation.

## 2. THE ROUTINE AT A GLANCE

### The core routine for a generally healthy adult

| Domain | Core target | Evidence | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dietary pattern | Mediterranean/DASH-like; mostly minimally processed plants, with flexible use of fish, dairy, eggs, poultry, and lean meat | **HIGH** for cardiometabolic risk; **HIGH** for cardiovascular events in high-risk populations | No single named diet is required |
| Vegetables + fruit | At least ~400 g/day; make vegetables the larger share | **HIGH** as guideline convergence; **MODERATE** for hard outcomes | Frozen and low-sodium canned options count |
| Fiber | At least 25 g/day; often 25–38 g/day or ~14 g/1,000 kcal | **HIGH** for risk factors; **MODERATE-HIGH** for long-term outcomes | Increase gradually and drink to thirst |
| Protein | Usually ~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for an active adult; ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day during resistance training, energy restriction, or later life | **HIGH** for training adaptation; **MODERATE** for a universal “optimal” target | 0.8 g/kg/day is the adult RDA for adequacy; kidney disease requires individual advice |
| Fat quality | Prefer olive/canola oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, and fish; keep saturated fat below ~10% of energy | **HIGH** | Replace saturated fat with unsaturated fat or high-fiber foods, not refined carbohydrate |
| Carbohydrate quality | Emphasize intact whole grains, legumes, fruit, and vegetables | **HIGH** | Total carbohydrate can vary with preference, glucose control, and training volume |
| Sodium | Generally <2,300 mg/day; WHO uses <2,000 mg/day | **HIGH** for blood pressure | Heavy sweaters, heat exposure, and some medical conditions require individualized fluid/electrolyte plans |
| Added sugar | Lower is better; keep below 10% of energy and make sugar-sweetened drinks rare | **HIGH** | Naturally occurring sugar in intact fruit is not equivalent to added sugar |
| Alcohol | Zero is the lowest-risk default; if consumed, less is better | **HIGH** for cancer causation; **MODERATE-HIGH** overall | Do not start drinking for health |
| Aerobic activity | 150–300 min/week moderate, or 75–150 min/week vigorous, or a mixture | **HIGH** | Build gradually; spread across the week |
| Strength | All major muscle groups on at least 2 days/week | **HIGH** for strength/function; **MODERATE** for mortality | One challenging set works; 2–3 sets generally produce more adaptation |
| Daily movement | Work toward ~7,000–9,000 steps/day if step counting is useful | **MODERATE** | Benefits begin far below 10,000; no magic threshold |
| Sitting | Break long sitting bouts with 2–5 minutes of walking or movement about every 30–60 minutes | **MODERATE** for acute metabolic outcomes | The exact interval is not a proven longevity threshold |
| Balance/power | Balance 2–3 days/week if older or at fall risk; add safe, intentional speed/power work when appropriate | **HIGH** for fall-prevention exercise; **MODERATE** for power training | Start with support and qualified supervision if fall risk is meaningful |
| Mobility | Train the ranges of motion needed for life and exercise, usually 5–10 minutes on most days | **HIGH** for improving range of motion | Stretching is useful, but not a substitute for strength or aerobic work |

### Minimum, target, and optional tiers

| Tier | Diet | Exercise |
|---|---|---|
| **Start here** | Add one vegetable/fruit serving, replace one refined snack or processed-meat meal, drink water instead of one sugary drink | Walk 10 minutes most days and complete one simple full-body strength session weekly |
| **Core health target** | Follow the food pattern and quantitative anchors in this report most of the time | 150–300 moderate-equivalent minutes, 2 strength days, ordinary daily movement |
| **Higher-benefit target** | Improve food quality, fiber, and energy balance without nutritional rigidity | If recovery permits, 300–600 min/week moderate or 150–300 vigorous-equivalent minutes is associated with near-maximal mortality benefit |
| **Optional optimization** | Earlier eating window, performance nutrition, measured protein distribution | One interval session, a third strength session, power work, sport-specific conditioning |

The higher-benefit exercise tier is based mainly on prospective cohorts, not on a decades-long randomized trial. It is an option, not a moral requirement.

## 3. THE DIET OPERATING SYSTEM

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for the overall pattern; MODERATE for the exact serving schedule.** The schedule below operationalizes Mediterranean, DASH, WHO, and current U.S. guidance. It should not be mistaken for proof that each serving count independently extends life.

### 3.1 Build most meals from four components

1. **Vegetables or fruit:** roughly half the plate, with vegetables the default at lunch and dinner.
2. **Protein:** beans, lentils, tofu/tempeh, fish, unsweetened dairy, eggs, poultry, or lean unprocessed meat.
3. **High-fiber carbohydrate:** oats, barley, brown rice, quinoa, potatoes, corn, or genuinely whole-grain bread/pasta.
4. **Unsaturated fat and flavor:** extra-virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado, herbs, spices, vinegar, or citrus.

Athletes and highly active people can enlarge the carbohydrate portion. Someone with low energy needs can enlarge non-starchy vegetables and keep energy-dense fats measured. A person managing diabetes can use the same pattern with individualized carbohydrate amounts and medication planning.

### 3.2 Practical food-frequency targets

| Food | Practical target | Examples and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetables | ≥3 servings/day | Include leafy greens and other colors; frozen is fine |
| Fruit | ~2 servings/day | Prefer intact fruit to juice |
| Whole grains/starchy whole foods | ~2–4 servings/day, adjusted for activity | Oats, barley, brown rice, quinoa, whole-grain bread, corn, potatoes |
| Legumes/soy | ≥3 servings/week; daily is reasonable | Beans, lentils, chickpeas, peas, tofu, tempeh |
| Nuts/seeds | ~1 oz (28 g) on most days if energy needs allow | Choose unsalted; nut butter counts |
| Fish/seafood | ~2 servings/week, especially oily fish | Salmon, sardines, trout, herring; follow local mercury advisories |
| Fermented or calcium-rich foods | Optional daily, based on tolerance/preferences | Plain yogurt/kefir, calcium-set tofu, fortified alternatives, some fermented vegetables |
| Eggs/poultry/lean meat | Flexible | Use as alternatives, not additions to an already energy-excess diet |
| Processed meat | Ideally rare | Bacon, sausage, deli meat, hot dogs; “processed” is the key distinction |
| Sugary drinks | Ideally near zero | Water, sparkling water, unsweetened coffee/tea are default options |
| Sweets/refined snacks | Occasional, portioned | A sustainable diet can include them; they should not displace core foods |

### 3.3 Protein: enough to protect function, not a contest

The adult RDA of **0.8 g/kg/day** is an adequacy floor derived to meet the needs of nearly all healthy adults; it was not designed as a muscle-maximization target. A practical health range is:

| Situation | Working range |
|---|---|
| Healthy, sedentary adult with stable weight | ~0.8–1.0 g/kg/day |
| Regularly active adult | ~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day |
| Resistance training, intentional fat loss, or healthy older adult | ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day |
| Acute/chronic illness, frailty, kidney disease, or major athletic training | Individualized with a qualified clinician/dietitian |

The best training meta-analyses find diminishing returns around **1.5–1.6 g/kg/day** for strength or lean-mass gains. More is not automatically harmful in a healthy person, but it is not established as better for long-term health and can crowd out fiber-rich foods.

For people with obesity, calculating a high protein target from current total body weight can produce an unnecessarily large number; goal weight, adjusted weight, diet history, and clinical context are more useful. For older adults or during weight loss, spreading protein across 3–4 eating occasions is reasonable. A practical meal target is often 25–40 g, but body size and total daily intake matter more than a perfect per-meal number.

**Source quality matters.** Make beans, lentils, soy, nuts, seeds, and fish routine protein sources. Prospective substitution studies generally favor replacing processed meat—and often some unprocessed red meat—with legumes, nuts, whole grains, or fish. This is not evidence that all animal foods are equivalent or that a vegan diet is mandatory.

### 3.4 Fat: replacement is the whole story

Keep saturated fat below about **10% of total energy** as a general ceiling. The strongest intervention evidence supports replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat; Mediterranean-pattern trials support extra-virgin olive oil and nuts as part of the full pattern.

Prefer:

- extra-virgin olive oil for dressings and most cooking;
- canola and other unsaturated oils when appropriate;
- nuts, seeds, avocado, and fish;
- foods containing fat in a minimally processed matrix.

Limit rather than demonize:

- butter, ghee, coconut oil, tallow, fatty/processed meats, and large amounts of high-saturated-fat dairy;
- deep-fried foods and energy-dense packaged snacks.

The claim that ordinary “seed oils” are uniquely toxic at culinary intakes is not supported by human outcome trials. Long-duration randomized evidence points in the opposite direction when polyunsaturated oils replace saturated fat. Repeatedly overheated restaurant frying oil is a different exposure from fresh oil used normally at home.

### 3.5 Carbohydrate: quality before percentage

No single carbohydrate percentage has been shown to be optimal for everyone. Low-carbohydrate and low-fat approaches can both support weight loss and glucose control when they improve food quality and reduce energy intake. The durable common ground is:

- obtain most carbohydrate from legumes, intact or minimally processed whole grains, fruit, vegetables, and tubers;
- reach at least **25 g/day of naturally occurring fiber**, with 25–38 g/day or ~14 g/1,000 kcal a useful working range;
- limit refined grains, sweets, and sugar-sweetened beverages;
- increase fiber gradually if current intake is low.

Whole fruit is not metabolically equivalent to fruit juice. Juice removes much of the intact structure, is rapidly consumed, and is easier to overconsume.

### 3.6 Energy balance and body composition

Chronic energy excess increases adiposity and cardiometabolic risk; chronic under-fueling can reduce muscle, bone, reproductive function, immunity, and training quality. The practical goal is not lifelong calorie counting. It is a stable feedback loop:

1. Track body-weight trend and waist or clothing fit for 2–4 weeks, not day-to-day noise.
2. If unwanted fat gain is occurring, first reduce liquid calories, alcohol, large restaurant portions, frequent snacks, and excess added fats/refined foods.
3. If strength, mood, sleep, libido/menstrual function, or training performance is falling while weight drops, increase energy intake and reassess the plan.
4. During intentional weight loss, keep resistance training and adequate protein in the program.

Sustained calorie restriction in non-obese adults improves many cardiometabolic risk factors, but it also reduces lean mass and bone density. It is not a universal prescription for already-lean, older, pregnant, growing, frail, or eating-disorder-prone people.

### 3.7 Meal timing and fasting

Meal timing is a secondary lever.

- A consistent **10–12-hour daytime eating window** is a reasonable default if it fits daily life.
- Avoiding very large meals close to bedtime may help sleep, reflux, and energy control.
- Earlier time-restricted eating has promising short-term metabolic data, but the strongest long-term synthesis finds intermittent fasting broadly similar to continuous energy restriction.
- A 16:8 schedule is not inherently superior, and skipping breakfast is not required.
- Fasting is a poor fit for pregnancy, a history of eating disorder, underweight/frailty, some diabetes regimens, or high training demands without professional planning.

Fasting can be a useful adherence structure. It is not evidence that calories, food quality, protein, or training recovery no longer matter.

### 3.8 Hydration and electrolytes

For most healthy people in temperate conditions, **thirst plus routine access to water is an adequate guide**. The National Academies' Adequate Intake values—3.7 L/day for men and 2.7 L/day for women—refer to **total water from food and all beverages**, not a requirement to drink that much plain water.

Drink more with heat, altitude, fever, pregnancy/lactation, or prolonged exercise. Sodium replacement becomes relevant with long-duration exercise or high sweat loss, not as a reason for the general population to ignore sodium guidance. Kidney, heart, and endocrine conditions can require fluid restriction or a specific electrolyte plan.

### 3.9 Supplements

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that food should be the foundation; LOW-to-MODERATE for routine supplements in nutrient-replete adults.**

Use supplements to solve an identified need, not as insurance against a poor routine.

Common legitimate indications include:

- vitamin B12 for vegan diets and some absorption/medication-related situations;
- folic acid before and during early pregnancy as clinically recommended;
- vitamin D, iron, calcium, iodine, or other nutrients when intake, laboratory testing, life stage, or a clinician identifies a need;
- creatine for strength/performance or selected older adults, if medically appropriate—useful, but not a substitute for training (Chilibeck et al., 2017, PMID: 29138605).

Routine high-dose antioxidant supplementation has not prevented cardiovascular disease or cancer. The USPSTF recommends against beta-carotene or vitamin E for that purpose. Large VITAL analyses did not find that vitamin D 2,000 IU/day prevented fractures in generally healthy, non-deficient midlife and older adults. COSMOS cognitive substudies reported a small multivitamin-associated cognitive signal in older adults, but no reduction in incident mild cognitive impairment or dementia was established (Sachs et al., 2023, PMID: 37035889); this is not a reason to treat a multivitamin as a longevity drug.

### 3.10 Alcohol

Alcohol is not a health food. Better-controlled meta-analyses do not show a reliable all-cause mortality advantage for low-volume drinking, and alcohol causally increases risk for at least seven cancers. The health-optimal default is **not drinking**. Someone who chooses to drink should drink less, avoid binge patterns, and never use alcohol as a cardiovascular intervention.

## 4. THE EXERCISE OPERATING SYSTEM

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that a mixed program improves health and function.** The exact weekly arrangement is a practical synthesis; no trial has randomized every healthy adult to this precise schedule for life.

### 4.1 Aerobic volume

The guideline target for adults is:

- **150–300 minutes/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity**, or
- **75–150 minutes/week of vigorous activity**, or
- an equivalent mixture, spread across the week.

Use the convention that one vigorous minute is approximately two moderate minutes when totaling guideline-equivalent volume.

Long-term cohort evidence suggests nearly maximal mortality association around **300–600 moderate minutes/week** or **150–300 vigorous minutes/week**. That upper range is optional. It is not necessary for a beginner, and it should not displace strength, sleep, recovery, or enjoyment.

### 4.2 Intensity without gadget confusion

| Intensity | Talk test | 0–10 effort (RPE) | Typical purpose |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Light | Easy conversation | 2–3 | Recovery, errands, sitting breaks |
| Moderate | Can speak in sentences, not sing comfortably | 4–6 | Most weekly aerobic volume |
| Vigorous | Only short phrases | 7–8 | Time-efficient fitness stimulus |
| Very hard interval | A few words; deliberately brief | 8–9 | Optional VO2-focused work |
| All-out | Unsustainable | 10 | Rarely needed for health; higher fatigue/injury cost |

“Zone 2” is an imprecise label across different heart-rate and lactate models. For general health, a sustainable moderate effort guided by breathing and talk test is sufficient. A watch estimate is useful for consistency, not a biological truth.

### 4.3 Strength training

Train all major muscle groups **at least twice per week**. A simple session includes these patterns:

1. knee-dominant: squat, sit-to-stand, leg press, step-up;
2. hip-dominant: deadlift pattern, hip hinge, bridge/hip thrust;
3. horizontal push: push-up, chest press;
4. horizontal pull: row;
5. vertical push: overhead press or appropriate substitute;
6. vertical pull: pulldown or assisted pull-up;
7. calf/ankle work;
8. trunk/carry: loaded carry, anti-rotation press, controlled trunk work.

For most adults:

- begin with **1–2 work sets** per exercise and progress toward 2–3;
- use loads that allow roughly **6–15 controlled repetitions** for most movements;
- finish most sets with **1–3 good repetitions still possible**;
- rest long enough to perform the next set with good technique, often 1–3 minutes;
- use machines, free weights, bands, or body weight—the resistance and progression matter more than the brand of tool.

Heavier loads (roughly ≥80% of one-repetition maximum) tend to maximize strength, while a wide range of loads can build muscle when sets are sufficiently challenging. Training to complete muscular failure is **not required** and creates more fatigue. Beginners, older adults, and people training alone generally have no reason to make failure the default.

### 4.4 Intervals

Intervals are an optional way to improve cardiorespiratory fitness efficiently. Add them only after several weeks of comfortable moderate training.

A conservative health-oriented session:

1. warm up easily for 8–10 minutes;
2. complete 4 repetitions of 2–4 minutes at RPE 8/10;
3. recover easily for an equal or slightly shorter time between repetitions;
4. cool down for 5–10 minutes.

One such session weekly is enough to begin. Cycling, rowing, incline walking, swimming, or an elliptical can reduce impact. HIIT improves fitness, but the five-year Generation 100 trial did not prove that prescribed HIIT lowers mortality compared with national activity guidance; the primary comparison was null, and subgroup comparisons were exploratory.

### 4.5 Daily movement and steps

Steps are a behavior tool, not a complete exercise program. The 2025 dose-response meta-analysis found that, compared with 2,000 steps/day, **7,000 steps/day** was associated with substantially lower risks across several outcomes; the evidence remains prospective and observational. Benefits began well below 10,000, with inflection points around 5,000–7,000 for several outcomes.

A reasonable sequence is:

- establish current average for one week;
- add 500–1,000 steps/day when comfortable;
- use 7,000–9,000 as a practical range, not a pass/fail score;
- go higher for enjoyment or training, not because 10,000 is biologically mandatory.

### 4.6 Break up sitting

Short walking breaks lower post-meal glucose and insulin exposure in acute randomized crossover trials. A 2026 meta-analysis of 39 trials found the largest acute effects with frequent walking breaks, often every 15–20 minutes. That frequency is difficult for many work settings and has not been shown to extend life.

The practical compromise is **2–5 minutes of walking, stairs, or light movement every 30–60 minutes**, especially after meals or during long desk periods. Standing alone is less consistently effective than moving.

### 4.7 Balance, power, and mobility

**Balance:** Adults 65+ with poor mobility or fall risk should perform balance-focused work on at least 3 days/week. Useful options include tandem stance/walk, single-leg stance with support, controlled step-overs, tai chi, and clinically designed programs such as Otago. Exercise reduces fall rate and may reduce fall-related fractures, though fracture evidence is less certain.

**Power:** Daily function depends on producing force quickly—rising from a chair, catching a trip, climbing stairs. Once strength technique is stable, appropriate adults can perform the lifting phase of selected low-risk movements intentionally fast while lowering under control. Power training shows a small functional advantage over traditional slow lifting in older adults, but certainty is low and supervision may be appropriate.

**Mobility:** Stretching reliably increases range of motion. Use it for joints or tasks that are actually limited. Dynamic range-of-motion work fits well in a warm-up; longer static holds fit after training or separately. Mobility work does not need to be elaborate, and resistance training through a comfortable full range also develops usable mobility.

## 5. A PRACTICAL WEEK

### 5.1 Core weekly schedule

This example produces a balanced health stimulus without requiring daily hard training.

| Day | Main work | Supporting movement |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Full-body strength A, 40–60 min | Easy 10–20 min walk |
| Tuesday | Moderate aerobic work, 35–50 min | Brief mobility if useful |
| Wednesday | Brisk walk or other moderate activity, 25–40 min | Balance practice or recreational movement |
| Thursday | Full-body strength B, 40–60 min | Easy walk |
| Friday | Optional intervals, including warm-up/cool-down, 25–40 min | Otherwise use moderate steady activity |
| Saturday | Longer easy/moderate session, sport, hike, bike, or swim, 45–90 min | Enjoyable activity counts |
| Sunday | Recovery: light walking and normal daily movement | Prepare food and schedule the next week |

If Friday includes 12 minutes of vigorous intervals, count those as roughly 24 moderate-equivalent minutes. Daily walking can supply the remainder of the 150–300-minute target.

### 5.2 Two simple strength sessions

Choose one exercise from each row. Perform 1–3 work sets, generally 6–15 repetitions, stopping with good form and 1–3 repetitions in reserve.

| Pattern | Session A | Session B |
|---|---|---|
| Knee | Goblet squat or leg press | Split squat or step-up |
| Hip | Romanian deadlift or hip hinge | Hip thrust or bridge |
| Push | Push-up or chest press | Overhead press or incline press |
| Pull | Cable/band/dumbbell row | Pulldown or assisted pull-up |
| Lower leg | Standing calf raise | Seated calf raise or controlled heel/toe walk |
| Trunk/carry | Farmer carry | Anti-rotation press or side plank |
| Balance/power, if appropriate | Supported single-leg stance | Fast-but-controlled sit-to-stand or low step-up |

Painful movements should be modified, not “pushed through.” The goal is to train the pattern with a tolerable variation.

### 5.3 Beginner on-ramp: first eight weeks

| Weeks | Aerobic | Strength | Main objective |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | 10–15 min easy-to-moderate, 4–5 days | 1 session, 1 set per pattern | Establish consistency and symptom-free movement |
| 3–4 | 15–25 min, 4–5 days | 2 sessions, 1 set per pattern | Reach ~90–120 min/week |
| 5–6 | 25–35 min, 4–5 days | 2 sessions, 1–2 sets | Reach ≥150 moderate minutes |
| 7–8 | Maintain volume; make one short segment vigorous if appropriate | 2 sessions, 2 sets on key movements | Begin gradual load progression |

Someone starting from a very low baseline may need more than eight weeks. That is normal.

### 5.4 A repeatable day of eating

This is a template, not a mandatory menu.

**Breakfast options**

- oats, berries, plain yogurt or fortified soy yogurt, walnuts, and ground flax;
- eggs or tofu with vegetables, beans, and whole-grain toast;
- plain Greek yogurt or soy yogurt with fruit, nuts, and high-fiber cereal.

**Lunch options**

- lentil/bean and whole-grain bowl with several vegetables and olive-oil dressing;
- large salad with chickpeas, salmon, tofu, or chicken, plus whole-grain bread or potatoes;
- leftovers built from the plate template.

**Dinner options**

- salmon, trout, tofu, tempeh, beans, or poultry; two vegetables; a whole grain or potato; olive oil/herbs;
- bean-and-vegetable chili with avocado and a whole-grain side;
- vegetable-heavy stir-fry with tofu/chicken, brown rice, and an unsaturated cooking oil.

**Snacks, if genuinely useful**

- fruit and nuts;
- vegetables and hummus;
- plain yogurt;
- whole-grain toast with nut butter.

Snacking is optional. A person who reaches energy and nutrient needs in meals does not need to manufacture extra eating occasions.

### 5.5 Around exercise

- A normal mixed meal within a few hours before and after ordinary training is sufficient.
- After strength training, obtaining a meaningful protein serving within several hours is reasonable; there is no minute-by-minute “anabolic window.”
- Carbohydrate before or after training becomes more important as session duration, intensity, or frequency increases.
- Fasted exercise has no established unique long-term health benefit. Use it only if comfortable and performance/recovery remain good.
- For sessions under about an hour in ordinary conditions, water and normal meals are usually sufficient. Prolonged heat/endurance work needs a specific fluid, sodium, and carbohydrate strategy.

## 6. HOW TO PROGRESS WITHOUT BREAKING THE ROUTINE

### 6.1 Aerobic progression

Progress in this order:

1. **frequency:** create regular days;
2. **duration:** extend easy/moderate sessions;
3. **intensity:** only then add vigorous segments or intervals.

Do not increase every variable at once. A new runner has a larger musculoskeletal loading jump than a new cyclist even when heart rate is similar.

### 6.2 Strength progression

Use a “rep range” method:

1. Select a load you can perform for the low end of the range with 2–3 repetitions in reserve.
2. Add repetitions over subsequent sessions while technique remains stable.
3. When all sets reach the top of the range, add a small amount of resistance and return to the lower end.
4. If pain, technique breakdown, or prolonged recovery appears, reduce the load, sets, range, or exercise complexity.

The 2026 network meta-analysis of advanced lifting methods found no clear superiority of complicated techniques over traditional resistance training for strength or power. Consistent progressive training is the main event.

### 6.3 Recovery checks

Temporarily reduce training load when several of these persist:

- performance falls across multiple sessions;
- unusual resting fatigue, irritability, or sleep disruption;
- persistent soreness or joint pain;
- loss of appetite or repeated illness;
- menstrual disruption, low libido, or other signs of low energy availability;
- motivation collapses rather than fluctuates normally.

A lighter week is not lost fitness. It is load management.

## 7. PERSONALIZATION BY GOAL AND LIFE STAGE

### 7.1 Fat loss

- Create a modest, sustainable energy deficit through food quality, portions, and environment.
- Keep protein toward ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day using an appropriate reference weight.
- Strength train at least twice weekly to preserve lean mass.
- Use aerobic activity and steps for health, work capacity, and energy expenditure, but do not rely on exercise alone to cancel unlimited intake.
- Slow or pause the deficit if strength, bone health, menstrual function, sleep, or mood deteriorates.

### 7.2 Muscle and strength gain

- Train each major muscle group at least twice weekly with progressive overload.
- Use multiple challenging sets and include some heavier work if strength is the goal.
- Consume ~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day protein; gains above this range are small or absent on average.
- Maintain enough carbohydrate and total energy to support training.
- A large calorie surplus is unnecessary and mostly increases fat gain.

### 7.3 Endurance performance

- Keep the health pattern but increase high-quality carbohydrate around training.
- Do not force a high-fiber, high-fat meal immediately before hard or long sessions if it causes gastrointestinal problems.
- Practice event fueling during training.
- Develop an individual sweat/fluid plan for prolonged heat exposure.
- Keep 1–2 strength sessions to support tissue capacity and function.

### 7.4 Adults over 65

Priorities shift toward preserving function:

- protein at least ~1.0–1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults, often toward 1.2 g/kg or higher with resistance training or recovery needs;
- strength at least twice weekly;
- balance work ≥3 days/week when mobility is poor or fall risk exists;
- appropriately supervised power practice;
- adequate calcium, vitamin D, B12, energy, and overall food intake;
- avoid aggressive calorie restriction unless there is a clear clinical reason and a plan to protect muscle and bone.

Resistance training improves strength and physical performance even in older adults with sarcopenia. Low volumes produce meaningful gains, which is useful when recovery or tolerance is limited.

### 7.5 Vegetarian and vegan diets

A well-designed vegetarian or vegan diet can fit this framework. For vegan diets:

- reliable vitamin B12 supplementation is non-negotiable;
- plan protein variety and total intake rather than obsessing over combining proteins at every meal;
- monitor or deliberately provide iron, zinc, iodine, calcium, vitamin D, and long-chain omega-3 sources as appropriate;
- use fortified foods and targeted supplements where the diet cannot reliably meet needs.

### 7.6 Chronic disease, pregnancy, and medication use

Diabetes, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, gastrointestinal disease, pregnancy, eating disorders, and medications that affect glucose, blood pressure, fluid balance, or heart rate can materially change this plan. These are not minor “biohacks”; they are reasons to individualize with the relevant clinician and dietitian.

## 8. WHAT TO MONITOR

Monitoring should improve decisions, not create anxiety.

### Weekly or monthly

- completion of planned aerobic and strength sessions;
- average steps if useful;
- body-weight trend if weight change is a goal;
- waist or clothing fit every 1–3 months;
- pain, fatigue, sleep, menstrual function, and training performance.

### Every 8–12 weeks

- a repeatable submaximal aerobic benchmark: same walking route, cycling workload, or similar test at the same perceived effort;
- key strength movements: repetitions and load with standardized technique;
- a functional benchmark when relevant: 30-second chair stand, usual gait speed, or a clinician-selected test.

### Clinical monitoring

Follow age-, risk-, and clinician-appropriate screening for blood pressure, lipids, glucose/HbA1c, kidney function, anemia/iron, B12, vitamin D, or bone density. More testing is not inherently better. Consumer biological-age clocks, continuous glucose monitors in metabolically healthy people, and frequent hormone panels have not been shown to improve long-term outcomes simply by being measured.

### When the plan is working

Over months, expect some combination of:

- lower effort or heart rate at the same aerobic workload;
- more strength or repetitions with stable technique;
- improved energy, sleep, and daily function;
- stable or intentionally changing weight/waist;
- an eating pattern that requires less daily negotiation;
- no persistent injury, under-fueling, or social isolation caused by the routine.

## 9. DIET EVIDENCE DEEP DIVE

### 9.1 Mediterranean pattern and cardiovascular events

> **Evidence quality: HIGH in people at elevated cardiovascular risk; MODERATE-HIGH for extrapolation to all healthy adults.**

**PREDIMED corrected republication** (Estruch et al., 2018, *New England Journal of Medicine*, PMID: 29897866) randomized 7,447 people at high cardiovascular risk to a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil, a Mediterranean diet supplemented with nuts, or advice to follow a low-fat diet. Both Mediterranean groups had fewer major cardiovascular events. The original publication was retracted and republished after randomization-procedure deviations were discovered; the corrected analyses remained favorable, but that history matters.

**CORDIOPREV** (Delgado-Lista et al., 2022, *Lancet*, PMID: 35525255) randomized 1,002 patients with established coronary heart disease to Mediterranean or low-fat dietary intervention for seven years. The primary composite event occurred in 87 Mediterranean-group participants and 111 low-fat participants; adjusted hazard ratios ranged from 0.719 to 0.753. It was single-center, 82.5% male, and supported partly by olive-oil-related organizations and Spanish public/EU funding.

These trials support a food pattern—not olive oil as a drug and not unlimited calorie intake from “healthy fats.”

### 9.2 DASH and sodium

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for blood-pressure reduction.**

The original DASH feeding trial (Appel et al., 1997, *New England Journal of Medicine*, PMID: 9099655) showed that a diet rich in fruit, vegetables, and low-fat dairy and lower in saturated fat reduced blood pressure, including in people without hypertension.

DASH-Sodium (Sacks et al., 2001, *New England Journal of Medicine*, PMID: 11136953) tested DASH versus control across three sodium levels in a controlled feeding design. Both the DASH pattern and sodium reduction lowered blood pressure, with the combination producing the largest effect. A 2025 crossover feeding trial in treated type 2 diabetes and hypertension (DASH4D; PMID: 40489102) again found clinically relevant blood-pressure lowering, driven primarily by sodium reduction.

Blood pressure is a validated cardiovascular risk factor, but a few weeks of feeding is not itself a mortality trial.

### 9.3 Ultra-processed diets

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that an ultra-processed menu can drive passive overconsumption under controlled conditions; MODERATE for attributing all long-term risk to processing itself.**

Hall et al. (2019, *Cell Metabolism*, PMID: 31105044) housed 20 adults in an inpatient unit and randomized them in crossover fashion to two-week ultra-processed and unprocessed diets matched on presented calories, macronutrients, sugar, sodium, and fiber. Participants ate about 500 kcal/day more on the ultra-processed diet and gained about 0.9 kg; they lost about 0.9 kg on the unprocessed diet.

This unusually strong experiment shows a causal effect of the tested menus on intake and short-term weight. It does **not** prove that every NOVA-classified processed food is harmful, that additives were the cause, or that nutritious convenience foods must be avoided. Energy density, eating rate, texture, palatability, and food structure are plausible contributors.

### 9.4 Fiber and carbohydrate quality

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for cardiometabolic intermediate outcomes; MODERATE-HIGH for prospective disease and mortality associations.**

Reynolds et al. (2019, *Lancet*, PMID: 30638909) integrated prospective cohorts and randomized trials. Higher fiber intake was associated with 15–30% lower all-cause/cardiovascular mortality and several diseases in observational data; trials showed lower body weight, systolic blood pressure, and cholesterol. The analysis supported at least 25–29 g/day, with higher intakes potentially providing additional benefit.

Fiber amount is not the whole diet. Isolated fiber added to a refined product may not reproduce the food matrix of legumes, whole grains, vegetables, fruit, nuts, and seeds.

### 9.5 Protein and muscle

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that protein and resistance training interact; MODERATE for exact health-span targets outside training.**

Morton et al. (2018, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID: 28698222) analyzed 49 resistance-training trials with 1,863 participants. Protein supplementation produced small additional gains in strength and fat-free mass; intakes above ~1.62 g/kg/day did not further increase training-induced fat-free mass on average.

Tagawa et al. (2022, *Sports Medicine Open*, PMID: 36057893) found that higher protein augmented strength only when combined with resistance training, with gains rising up to ~1.5 g/kg/day and no further average gain thereafter. The lead affiliations included a food company R&D division, which should be noted even though the result is consistent with independent work.

Prospective mortality syntheses generally favor plant protein, especially when it replaces processed meat or other less favorable sources (Naghshi et al., 2020, *BMJ*, PMID: 32699048). This remains observational substitution evidence, not a randomized lifespan trial.

### 9.6 Saturated fat and unsaturated replacement

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for LDL lowering; MODERATE-HIGH for cardiovascular events.**

The 2020 Cochrane review (Hooper et al., PMID: 32428300) included long-duration randomized trials and found that reducing saturated fat lowered combined cardiovascular events by 21% (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66–0.93), with no clear mortality effect. Replacement with polyunsaturated fat was the most consistently supported strategy.

This is why “saturated fat is bad” is incomplete. The correct statement is: **replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat reduces cardiovascular risk; replacing it with refined carbohydrate may not.**

### 9.7 Calorie restriction and aging biomarkers

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for weight and cardiometabolic changes; LOW-MODERATE for slowing human aging; no human lifespan result.**

CALERIE randomized 218 healthy non-obese adults to a prescribed 25% calorie reduction or usual intake for two years. Participants achieved about 11.9% restriction, lost ~7.5 kg, and improved LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, inflammation, insulin sensitivity, and metabolic-syndrome score (Kraus et al., 2019, *Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology*, PMID: 31303390).

A later analysis found a small slowing of DunedinPACE but not consistent change across all DNA-methylation clocks (Waziry et al., 2023, *Nature Aging*, PMID: 37118425). Clocks are surrogate measures, not proof of longer life.

Costs were real: fat-free mass and bone mineral density fell, and monitoring for bone loss and anemia was advised (Romashkan et al., 2016, PMID: 26992237; Villareal et al., 2016, PMID: 26332798). “Eat less forever” is therefore not a universal conclusion.

### 9.8 Intermittent fasting and time restriction

> **Evidence quality: MODERATE for weight loss as an adherence strategy; LOW for unique longevity effects.**

The 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis of 99 randomized trials and 6,582 adults found intermittent-fasting approaches broadly similar to continuous energy restriction for body weight and cardiometabolic risk factors. Alternate-day fasting had a small short-term weight advantage over continuous restriction (~1.29 kg), below the review's 2-kg threshold for a minimally important difference; long-duration evidence was sparse (Semnani-Azad et al., PMID: 40533200; corrected by PMID: 40825602).

In a 12-month trial, adding an 8 a.m.–4 p.m. eating window to calorie restriction did not produce greater weight loss than calorie restriction alone (Liu et al., 2022, *New England Journal of Medicine*, PMID: 35443107). A small, tightly controlled five-week trial in men with prediabetes found early time-restricted feeding improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure without weight loss (Sutton et al., 2018, PMID: 29754952). The signal is interesting but not a hard-outcome result or evidence that a 3 p.m. dinner is required.

### 9.9 Alcohol

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for dose-related harm and cancer causation; no high-quality evidence that starting low-dose alcohol improves health.**

A 2023 meta-analysis of 107 cohorts (4.8 million participants) that addressed former-drinker and other biases found no statistically significant mortality reduction for occasional or low-volume drinking versus lifetime non-drinking; risk rose at higher intakes and at lower intake in women (Zhao et al., *JAMA Network Open*, PMID: 37000449).

The U.S. Surgeon General and WHO/IARC identify a causal link between alcohol and cancers of the breast, colorectum, esophagus, liver, mouth, throat, and larynx. Cancer risk does not have a demonstrated harmless threshold.

## 10. EXERCISE EVIDENCE DEEP DIVE

### 10.1 Aerobic activity dose and mortality

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that activity improves health; MODERATE-HIGH for the exact mortality curve because it is observational.**

The U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines and WHO converge on 150–300 moderate or 75–150 vigorous minutes per week plus muscle strengthening on at least two days.

Lee et al. (2022, *Circulation*, PMID: 35876019) followed large U.S. cohorts with repeated activity measurements. Nearly maximal mortality association occurred around 150–300 vigorous or 300–600 moderate minutes/week. Compared with meeting the minimum guideline, 2–4 times the minimum was associated with only modest additional risk reduction. Self-reported activity and healthy-user confounding remain limitations.

### 10.2 Cardiorespiratory fitness

> **Evidence quality: MODERATE-HIGH as a prognostic marker; HIGH that training can improve fitness.**

An overview of 26 meta-analyses representing 20.9 million observations found high versus low measured cardiorespiratory fitness associated with roughly half the all-cause mortality risk; each 1-MET higher fitness was associated with an 11–17% lower risk. GRADE certainty ranged from very low to moderate because the mortality evidence is cohort-based (Lang et al., 2024, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID: 38599681).

Fitness is therefore one of the strongest measurable risk markers in preventive medicine. It is not correct to convert the cohort association into a guarantee that adding exactly 1 MET will cause a fixed lifespan gain.

### 10.3 Steps

> **Evidence quality: MODERATE. Device-measured prospective evidence is strong, but not randomized.**

Paluch et al. (2022, *Lancet Public Health*, PMID: 35247352) pooled 15 cohorts and found the risk curve flattened at roughly 6,000–8,000 steps/day in adults 60+ and 8,000–10,000 in younger adults; stepping rate added little after total volume adjustment.

A broader 2025 review of 57 studies found 7,000 versus 2,000 steps/day associated with lower all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, dementia, depressive symptoms, diabetes, and falls, with inflection points around 5,000–7,000 for several outcomes (Ding et al., *Lancet Public Health*, PMID: 40713949). These large associations may still include reverse causation: healthier people can walk more.

### 10.4 Resistance training prescription

> **Evidence quality: HIGH for strength and hypertrophy; MODERATE for mortality.**

Currier et al. (2023, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, PMID: 37414459) synthesized 178 strength studies and 119 hypertrophy studies. All tested resistance prescriptions outperformed no exercise. Higher loads maximized strength; multiple sets ranked highly for hypertrophy; twice- or thrice-weekly prescriptions performed well.

Training to failure is not required. Grgic et al. (2022, PMID: 33497853) found no overall difference between failure and non-failure training for strength or hypertrophy, with greater fatigue cost from failure training.

Mortality evidence is prospective. Momma et al. (2022, PMID: 35228201) found muscle-strengthening activity associated with 10–17% lower risks across several outcomes, with the largest observational reduction around 30–60 minutes/week. The apparent J-shape at higher volumes should not be read as proof that more strength training is harmful; exposure measurement and confounding limit the inference.

### 10.5 HIIT versus moderate continuous training

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that both improve fitness; LOW-MODERATE that one is superior for longevity.**

HIIT is time-efficient and often produces equal or somewhat larger VO2 improvements than moderate continuous training in short trials. Hard outcomes are much less certain.

Generation 100 randomized 1,567 adults aged 70–77 to twice-weekly HIIT, moderate continuous training, or national physical-activity guidance for five years. The combined supervised exercise groups did not have significantly lower all-cause mortality than the guideline group. HIIT versus moderate training favored HIIT numerically, but the comparison was exploratory and did not establish mortality superiority (Stensvold et al., 2020, *BMJ*, PMID: 33028588).

### 10.6 Balance and falls

> **Evidence quality: HIGH that appropriately designed exercise reduces falls.**

The 2019 Cochrane review included 108 randomized trials and found exercise reduced fall rate in community-dwelling older adults. Balance/functional exercise, often combined with resistance work, was particularly effective. Fall-related fracture reduction was possible but lower-certainty (Sherrington et al., PMID: 30703272).

### 10.7 Power and mobility

> **Evidence quality: MODERATE for function; HIGH for increased range of motion.**

A 2022 meta-analysis of 20 trials in older adults found power training produced a small improvement in physical function versus traditional strength training, but certainty was low because of bias and imprecision (Balachandran et al., *JAMA Network Open*, PMID: 35544136).

Stretch training reliably increases range of motion across techniques, ages, and training states (Konrad et al., 2024, *Journal of Sport and Health Science*, PMID: 37301370). Evidence that extra flexibility beyond task requirements independently extends life is absent.

### 10.8 Sitting breaks

> **Evidence quality: MODERATE for acute glucose/insulin effects; LOW for long-term clinical outcomes.**

A 2026 meta-analysis of 39 acute randomized crossover trials found activity breaks reduced post-meal glucose and insulin exposure; walking breaks were most effective, with the largest pooled changes at 15–20-minute intervals (Gale et al., *Obesity Reviews*, PMID: 42070794). These were short laboratory studies. A practical 30–60-minute movement reminder is an implementation compromise, not a proven medical threshold.

## 11. COMMON CLAIMS THAT OVERREACH THE EVIDENCE

| Claim | What the evidence actually supports |
|---|---|
| “There is one optimal human diet.” | Several high-quality patterns work. Their overlap—plants, fiber, minimally processed foods, unsaturated fats, appropriate energy—is more defensible than one macronutrient ideology. |
| “Everyone needs 1.6 g/kg protein.” | That intake is a useful upper working target for muscle adaptation, dieting, or some older adults. It is not proven necessary for every sedentary healthy adult. |
| “Animal protein is always harmful.” | Food sources differ. Processed meat is consistently least favorable; fish, yogurt, eggs, poultry, and red meat do not have identical evidence. Plant-forward substitution is the most defensible default. |
| “Seed oils are poisonous.” | Human trials support replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat. Evidence about repeatedly degraded frying oils should not be generalized to normal home use. |
| “Low-carb/keto is best for longevity.” | Low-carb diets can help selected people with weight or glucose control. No adequately controlled human longevity trial establishes superiority, and food sources determine much of the long-term risk signal. |
| “Fasting activates autophagy, so longer is better.” | Human autophagy is difficult to quantify in free-living whole bodies. Fasting can aid adherence; long-term randomized evidence shows broadly similar results to continuous restriction. |
| “Zone 2 is mandatory.” | Moderate continuous exercise is foundational. Exact zone boundaries vary by method, and hard-outcome trials do not require lactate testing or a specific wearable zone. |
| “HIIT is the best exercise for longevity.” | HIIT improves fitness efficiently. It has not proved mortality superiority over a good mixed program. |
| “10,000 steps is the minimum.” | Benefits begin at much lower counts; ~7,000 is a useful practical target for many adults. |
| “Exercise will automatically cause major weight loss.” | Exercise produces large health gains independent of weight, but average weight loss from exercise alone is often modest because intake and compensation change. |
| “Stretching prevents all injuries.” | Stretching improves range of motion. Injury prevention depends on load progression, strength, skill, fatigue, and sport demands; stretching alone is insufficient. |
| “A supplement can cover a weak diet.” | Supplements can correct specific gaps. They do not reproduce the food matrix, training stimulus, or cardiovascular effects of the overall routine. |
| “A lower biological-age result proves the routine works.” | Clock changes are surrogate outcomes. Function, fitness, blood pressure, lipids, glucose, body composition, adverse effects, and eventually clinical outcomes matter more. |

## 12. SAFETY AND MEDICAL BOUNDARIES

### Before increasing exercise

Most asymptomatic people can begin light activity and progress gradually without a medical stress test. The ACSM screening approach focuses on current activity, known cardiovascular/metabolic/renal disease, symptoms, and intended intensity—not simply on counting risk factors (Riebe et al., 2015, PMID: 26473759).

Obtain individualized clearance or programming before vigorous exercise when there is known cardiovascular, metabolic, or renal disease, significant orthopedic/neurologic limitation, pregnancy-related concern, or relevant symptoms.

### Stop exercise and seek prompt medical assessment for

- chest pressure, squeezing, or pain, especially if it radiates;
- fainting, near-fainting, or new confusion;
- unusual or severe shortness of breath;
- a new fast or irregular heartbeat with symptoms;
- sudden weakness, neurologic symptoms, or severe headache;
- severe or rapidly worsening pain;
- heat-stroke signs such as confusion, collapse, seizure, or hot skin with very high temperature.

Emergency symptoms warrant emergency services, not an online routine adjustment.

### Diet red flags

Pause aggressive dietary change and obtain help for:

- recurrent fainting, severe weakness, dehydration, or hypoglycemia;
- rapid unintended weight loss;
- persistent vomiting, diarrhea, bleeding, or inability to eat;
- menstrual loss, stress fractures, or other low-energy-availability signs;
- escalating food fear, bingeing, purging, compulsive exercise, or social withdrawal around food;
- suspected nutrient deficiency or medication–diet interaction.

## 13. EVIDENCE SUMMARY

### Strongest foundations

| Practice | Grade | What is established |
|---|---|---|
| Mediterranean/DASH-like pattern | **HIGH** | Lowers blood pressure and cardiovascular events in studied high-risk populations |
| Replace saturated with unsaturated fat | **HIGH** | Lowers LDL; long trials reduce combined cardiovascular events |
| High-fiber whole-food carbohydrate sources | **HIGH** | Improve cardiometabolic risk factors; strong prospective disease associations |
| Appropriate energy balance | **HIGH** | Prevents chronic over- or under-nutrition; weight loss improves risk in people with excess adiposity |
| 150–300 min moderate aerobic equivalent | **HIGH** | Broad health benefit across guidelines and convergent evidence |
| Strength ≥2 days/week | **HIGH** | Improves strength, muscle, function, metabolic health, and bone-related outcomes |
| Balance-focused exercise in older/fall-risk adults | **HIGH** | Reduces falls |
| No smoking; little or no alcohol | **HIGH** | Avoids major established disease risks; alcohol is a causal carcinogen |

### Useful but conditional

| Practice | Grade | Proper interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Protein 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day | **HIGH** for training; **MODERATE** universally | Useful for resistance training, dieting, and many older adults; not mandatory for everyone |
| 7,000–9,000 steps/day | **MODERATE** | Practical behavior target based on prospective device data |
| 300–600 moderate minutes/week | **MODERATE-HIGH** | Associated with near-maximal mortality benefit; optional if recovery is good |
| Weekly HIIT | **HIGH** for fitness; **LOW-MODERATE** for superiority | Efficient option, not a requirement |
| Earlier 10–12-hour eating window | **MODERATE** | Reasonable structure; unique long-term advantage unproven |
| Sitting breaks every 30–60 minutes | **MODERATE** | Practical extrapolation from acute metabolic trials |
| Power training in older adults | **MODERATE** | Small functional benefit; implement safely |

### Not established as longevity upgrades

- carnivore, ketogenic, or very-low-fat diets for the general population;
- prolonged fasting or routine multi-day fasts;
- exact wearable heart-rate zones or lactate-guided training for ordinary health;
- routine high-dose vitamins, antioxidants, NAD boosters, or “detox” products;
- daily maximal lifting, training to failure, or all-out intervals;
- consumer biological-age testing as the main success metric.

## FINAL SYNTHESIS

The evidence does not identify a secret protocol. It identifies a durable hierarchy:

1. **Do the basics consistently:** minimally processed, plant-forward meals; appropriate energy; regular aerobic activity; strength training; daily movement.
2. **Protect function:** muscle, aerobic capacity, balance, bone, and adequate nutrition become more—not less—important with age.
3. **Use optional tools for a specific reason:** intervals for fitness efficiency, time restriction for adherence, supplements for documented gaps, tracking for decisions.
4. **Do not confuse biomarkers with outcomes:** a lower glucose spike, LDL value, scale weight, or biological-age estimate can be informative, but the goal is longer, healthier, more capable life.
5. **The optimal routine is the strongest evidence you can recover from and repeat.**
