# Human Optimization: An Evidence-Based Operating System

**Last verified: August 23, 2026 | Scope: generally healthy adults; health, physical capacity, appearance, energy, and long-term maintenance**

This is the umbrella routine for taking care of a human body. It is deliberately broader than personal care and deliberately narrower than the biohacking market. The core contains practices that are broadly applicable, repeatable, supported by meaningful human evidence or established preventive guidance, and worth their burden.

It is not a disease-treatment protocol. A diagnosed condition, pregnancy, disability, medication, eating-disorder history, or major symptom can change the right exercise, diet, supplement, skin, and preventive plan. Clinician instructions override this guide.

## The Filter

An item belongs in the core only when it has:

1. **Broad applicability** to a generally healthy adult.
2. **A meaningful outcome** such as function, fitness, strength, sleep, dental health, appearance, quality of life, or prevention—not merely a transient biomarker.
3. **Credible human evidence**, preferably replicated trials, systematic reviews, or evidence-based public-health guidance.
4. **A favorable tradeoff** among benefit, risk, cost, time, and adherence.
5. **A repeatable cadence** that can live in an ordinary week.

Evidence labels in this document:

- **HIGH** — replicated human outcome evidence or strong evidence-based guidance.
- **MODERATE** — credible human evidence, but with smaller effects, substantial observational evidence, or an uncertain ideal dose.
- **SUPPORT** — sensible hygiene or execution support without a large independent health effect.
- **OPTIONAL** — evidence may be good, but the intervention is goal-dependent rather than a universal requirement.

This filter excludes condition-specific drugs from the general routine, even when those drugs work well for the condition. It also excludes optimization theater: expensive measurement without a decision, supplements without a demonstrated need, and interventions promoted from mechanistic or biomarker evidence alone.

## The Short Version

| Domain | Operating target | Cadence | Evidence |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Sleep | Obtain at least 7 hours regularly and allow enough time for it; keep sleep and wake timing reasonably consistent | Daily | HIGH for duration; MODERATE for regularity |
| Aerobic activity | Accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate activity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity | Weekly | HIGH |
| Strength | Strengthen all major muscle groups across at least 2 days | 2+ days/week | HIGH |
| Everyday movement | Move more and sit less; short bouts count | Throughout day | HIGH |
| Food pattern | Base most meals on minimally processed, nutrient-dense foods; emphasize vegetables, fruit, pulses, whole grains, nuts/seeds, and adequate protein | Daily | HIGH |
| Fiber and produce | Aim for at least 400 g of vegetables and fruit and at least 25 g of naturally occurring fiber | Daily | HIGH |
| Protein | Meet at least the 0.8 g/kg/day adult floor; roughly 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day is a practical range when resistance training or prioritizing muscle | Daily | HIGH for training adaptation; not a universal longevity dose |
| Tobacco/nicotine | Do not start; eliminate tobacco exposure and prioritize cessation if currently using | Continuous | HIGH |
| Alcohol | Less is better; zero minimizes alcohol-attributable risk | Weekly average | HIGH for harm; no evidence-based need to drink |
| Oral care | Fluoride toothpaste for about two minutes twice daily; clean between teeth once daily | Daily | HIGH/MODERATE |
| Skin/appearance | Protect ordinarily exposed skin from UV; use simple cleansing and moisturizer according to need | Daily/exposure-based | HIGH for sunscreen; SUPPORT for basic care |
| Relationships | Protect recurring time for high-quality contact, belonging, and mutual support | Recurring | MODERATE; much evidence is observational |
| Preventive care | Keep vaccinations, evidence-based screening, dental care, and clinician-set risk checks current | Clinician-set/annual review | HIGH |

These are floors and ranges, not a contest. Going from none to some usually matters more than perfecting the last few percent.

## The Daily Operating Routine

### After Waking

1. **Wake at a reasonably stable time.** Do not sacrifice adequate duration merely to preserve an exact alarm time. Adults should regularly obtain at least seven hours of sleep; sleep need varies, and many function best with roughly seven to nine ([AASM/SRS consensus, PMID 26039963](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26039963/)). Evidence supports regular timing, but does not establish a universal minute-level target ([systematic review, PMID 33054339](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33054339/)).
2. **Optionally use daylight and movement to support the schedule.** Outdoor morning light can help anchor circadian timing; brighter morning light is associated with earlier sleep timing, while brighter evening light tends to delay it. The evidence in healthy adults is heterogeneous and not strong enough to make this a required anti-aging habit or prescribe a universal number of minutes ([systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6814154/)). A short walk is an easy experiment that combines light and movement.
3. **Brush with fluoride toothpaste for about two minutes.** Use a soft manual or powered brush. Technique and consistency matter more than price ([ADA home oral care](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/home-oral-care/)).
4. **Use UV protection on ordinarily exposed skin.** Apply a broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen to exposed face, ears, neck, scalp/hairline, and hands when UV exposure is expected. Clothing, shade, and hats reduce the sunscreen burden. Daily use slowed objectively scored photoaging in a 4.5-year randomized trial; the result was 24% lower odds of an increased photoaging score, not “24% fewer wrinkles” ([PMID 23732711](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23732711/)).

### Through the Day

1. **Break up long sitting and accumulate movement.** Walking, stairs, errands, brief activity breaks, and training all count. U.S. guidance explicitly says to move more, sit less, and count activity of any bout length ([Physical Activity Guidelines](https://odphp.health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines/top-10-things-know)).
2. **Build meals rather than chase superfoods.** Most meals should contain:

   - a meaningful protein source;
   - vegetables and/or fruit;
   - a high-fiber carbohydrate such as beans, lentils, intact whole grains, or starchy vegetables when useful;
   - an unsaturated-fat source such as nuts, seeds, olive oil, avocado, or fish;
   - portions that support stable energy, training, and the desired body-composition trend.

   Healthy diets can take many cultural forms. The durable principles are adequacy, balance, moderation, diversity, and a base of minimally processed foods ([WHO healthy diet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet), [2025–2030 U.S. Dietary Guidelines](https://odphp.health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/dietary-guidelines)).
3. **Use produce and fiber as concrete anchors.** A practical evidence-based floor is at least 400 g of vegetables and fruit and 25 g of naturally occurring fiber daily. Whole grains and pulses count toward the high-fiber pattern ([WHO carbohydrate guideline](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/370420/9789240073593-eng.pdf)). Increase fiber gradually if current intake is low.
4. **Get enough protein, especially when training.** The adult reference floor is 0.8 g/kg/day. For a healthy adult doing resistance training or prioritizing muscle retention/gain, roughly 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day is a useful range, distributed across meals. A 49-trial meta-analysis found small additional strength and lean-mass gains from protein supplementation during resistance training and no further fat-free-mass benefit beyond about 1.6 g/kg/day ([PMID 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)). Protein powder can be a convenient way to reach the total, but it is not nutritionally interchangeable with a varied meal or necessarily as filling. When current body weight is far from a healthy or intended range, do not mechanically multiply that weight by a target; a reference/target weight or dietitian-guided calculation may be more appropriate.
5. **Drink according to thirst, meals, activity, and environment.** Water is the default beverage. Increase intake with heat, prolonged exercise, illness, or heavy sweating. There is no evidence-based universal “gallon a day” target for healthy adults, and ordinary hydration is not a stimulant.

### Before Bed

1. **Clean between the teeth once daily and brush again with fluoride toothpaste.** Floss, an interdental brush, or a water flosser is acceptable when it fits the person's anatomy and is used consistently. Evidence does not show that one device is universally superior; daily interdental cleaning is the care standard ([ADA interdental guidance](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/floss)).
2. **Do the minimum effective skin routine.** Cleanse off sunscreen, sweat, and visible residue with a gentle cleanser. Apply a tolerated moisturizer where skin is dry or irritated. More products do not automatically produce better skin.
3. **Create a repeatable wind-down.** Reduce bright light and stimulating work near bedtime, make the room dark and quiet, and choose a comfortable temperature. These are execution supports; the high-value target is adequate, regular sleep.
4. **Do not use alcohol as a sleep aid.** Sedation is not the same as restorative sleep, and alcohol adds health risk rather than solving the cause of poor sleep.

## The Weekly Physical-Capacity Plan

The evidence-based public-health target is **150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, plus muscle strengthening on at least two days** ([U.S. Physical Activity Guidelines](https://odphp.health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines/top-10-things-know)). A mix of moderate and vigorous work is acceptable. “Zone 2,” intervals, running, cycling, swimming, sports, dancing, and brisk walking are tools—not uniquely mandatory protocols.

The longer diet and training rationale, progression rules, and life-stage adaptations are in [`DIET_AND_EXERCISE.md`](./DIET_AND_EXERCISE.md). This umbrella keeps only the actions needed to run an ordinary week.

### A Practical Default Week

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Monday | Full-body strength A, 30–60 minutes |
| Tuesday | Moderate aerobic work, 30–45 minutes |
| Wednesday | Easy movement or a brisk walk; no formal session required |
| Thursday | Full-body strength B, 30–60 minutes |
| Friday | Moderate aerobic work or brief vigorous intervals, according to fitness and preference |
| Saturday | Longer enjoyable aerobic activity, sport, hike, ride, swim, or dance |
| Sunday | Easy movement and recovery |

This is an implementation example, not a scientifically privileged split. Rearrange it around adherence and recovery.

### Strength Session Template

Cover the major functions over the week:

- knee-dominant lower body: squat, split squat, step-up, or leg press;
- hip-dominant lower body: hinge, deadlift variation, hip thrust, or hamstring curl;
- upper-body push: push-up, bench press, or overhead press;
- upper-body pull: row, pulldown, or pull-up;
- trunk and carry: loaded carry, anti-rotation, flexion/extension as tolerated;
- calves, arms, or other accessory work if appearance or sport goals justify it.

Use loads and ranges of motion that can be controlled. Add repetitions, load, range, or sets gradually when the work becomes easy. The universal requirement is progressive muscle strengthening, not a particular exercise, rep range, or barbell lift.

### Aerobic Session Template

- **Moderate:** breathing is clearly elevated but conversation remains possible.
- **Vigorous:** conversation is difficult and effort is high.
- **Progression:** increase frequency or duration before making every session hard.

One hard session can be useful for fitness and time efficiency, but repeated maximal work is not required for general health. Pain, illness, heat, deconditioning, and medications can change safe intensity.

### Mobility, Balance, and Power

Strength training through a comfortable range, varied movement, and a brief specific warm-up cover much of what a healthy younger adult needs. Add dedicated mobility only for a movement goal or restriction. Balance and power work become more important with age, falls risk, or sport demands; they are not a mandatory daily “mobility flow” for everyone.

## Nutrition Without Diet Theater

### What to Do Most of the Time

- Eat a diverse, minimally processed pattern that can be sustained.
- Make vegetables, fruit, pulses, whole grains, nuts, and seeds routine.
- Include adequate protein at meals.
- Prefer water and unsweetened drinks over sugar-sweetened drinks.
- Use mostly unsaturated fats and include fish if compatible with preference and diet.
- Limit foods dominated by refined starch, added sugar, sodium, and industrial processing without banning all convenience food.
- Match portions to activity, hunger, and multi-week body-composition trends.

### Body Composition

There is no universal ideal aesthetic weight or body-fat percentage. For appearance and health, the reliable tools are resistance training, adequate protein, food quality, energy balance, and time.

- For fat loss, use a modest, sustainable energy deficit that preserves training performance and protein intake.
- For muscle gain, progress resistance training; a large calorie surplus is unnecessary.
- Judge a plan over four to eight weeks using function, waist/weight trend if relevant, training performance, hunger, mood, and appearance—not a single day's scale reading.
- Do not pursue aggressive restriction when already lean, recovering poorly, or at risk for disordered eating.

### Supplements

There is no universal supplement stack. Use a supplement when it fills a demonstrated dietary gap, addresses a clinician-identified need, or has a specific goal with credible evidence. A multivitamin, vitamin D, omega-3 capsule, probiotic, iron, magnesium, B12, electrolyte mix, or “greens” powder should not be added automatically to a healthy adult's daily routine.

## Energy and Performance

Persistent energy should come from sleep, physical capacity, enough food, hydration, and recovery. Stimulants can improve alertness temporarily; they do not repay sleep debt.

### Optional Caffeine

> **OPTIONAL — useful for alertness or exercise, not a health requirement.**

- Use the lowest dose that reliably helps; a small coffee or tea is often enough.
- Treat 400 mg/day as a ceiling for most healthy adults, not a target. Individual sensitivity, pregnancy, medications, and some health conditions lower the appropriate amount ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)).
- Default to finishing caffeine at least eight hours before bed. Dose matters: in a small crossover trial, a single 400 mg dose altered sleep when taken as early as 12 hours before bed, while 100 mg did not significantly alter sleep at four hours in that study ([PMID 39377163](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39377163/)).
- Do not use bulk pure caffeine powder.
- If the dose keeps rising, first investigate sleep, workload, food intake, and health rather than adding another stimulant.

### Optional Creatine Monohydrate

> **OPTIONAL — one of the few well-supported performance supplements, mainly for strength, power, and resistance-training adaptation.**

- Plain creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g/day, is the standard maintenance approach; loading is optional, not necessary ([NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/)).
- The effect is additive to training, not a substitute for it. Evidence is strongest for repeated high-intensity performance, strength, and training adaptation. Increases in measured lean mass can include water, and a meta-analysis restricted to direct muscle imaging found at most a very small and uncertain additional hypertrophy effect ([PMID 37432300](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/)).
- Expect possible water-weight gain and occasional gastrointestinal discomfort.
- Creatine can raise serum creatinine and make creatinine-based kidney estimates look worse without showing injury on alternative measures. Tell the clinician interpreting kidney labs. Long-term evidence in kidney disease and pregnancy is not a basis for unsupervised use ([PMID 42507286](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42507286/)).
- Choose a plain product with credible independent quality testing; more expensive forms have not shown superiority.

### When Low Energy Is Not an Optimization Problem

Do not indefinitely self-treat persistent fatigue, falling performance, unexplained weight change, excessive sleepiness, loud snoring/gasping, shortness of breath, depressed mood, or exercise intolerance with caffeine and supplements. Those are reasons for clinical assessment, not a larger stack.

## Appearance and Personal Maintenance

Appearance is driven mostly by body composition, muscle, skin UV exposure, teeth, hair grooming, sleep, and consistency. The high-efficacy routine is short.

### Skin

- **Core:** broad-spectrum SPF 30+ on exposed skin, plus clothing/shade during substantial exposure.
- **Core support:** gentle cleansing when needed and moisturizer where dry or irritated.
- **Optional advanced appearance care:** prescription topical tretinoin can improve facial photodamage when tolerated; it is elective, often irritating, and belongs under a prescription plan. See the [personal-care routine](./PERSONAL_CARE.md#tretinoin-frequency).
- **Not core:** do not add cosmetic serums or devices to the general routine; their evidence is usually formulation- or device-specific and lower priority. See the [personal-care evidence review](./PERSONAL_CARE.md).

### Teeth and Breath

- Fluoride brushing twice daily and interdental cleaning once daily are the baseline.
- Regular dental care should follow individual risk, not an arbitrary universal six-month rule ([ADA](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/home-oral-care/)).
- Whitening, antimicrobial mouthwash, high-fluoride products, periodontal trays, and other adjuncts are optional or risk/condition-specific, not universal maintenance.

### Hair, Scalp, Body, Nails, and Feet

- Wash the scalp according to oil, sweat, styling products, and comfort; use conditioner when it improves detangling and breakage.
- Avoid repeated high heat, chemical damage, and sustained tight traction if hair preservation matters.
- Bathe according to sweat, odor, work, and skin tolerance; focus soap on areas that need it rather than aggressively stripping all skin.
- Keep nails and grooming tools clean; moisturize dry hands, body skin, or feet as needed.
- Hair-loss drugs, antifungal shampoos, acne drugs, medicated foot care, and periodontal treatments belong to a diagnosed problem or clinician plan, not the healthy-adult baseline.

The detailed cadence and evidence notes live in [`PERSONAL_CARE.md`](./PERSONAL_CARE.md). That document can hold individualized treatment modules without turning them into universal recommendations.

## Mental, Social, and Cognitive Maintenance

### Relationships and Belonging

Maintain more than passive online contact. Protect recurring time for people and communities where there is mutual care, useful contribution, and honest conversation. A standing dinner, activity group, team, volunteer commitment, or scheduled call is easier to sustain than a vague intention to “be social.” Evidence strongly links isolation and poor connection with worse health, but it does not establish a universal number of contacts or prove that any single social intervention extends life.

Learning and challenging hobbies can be worthwhile for enjoyment and function, but no activity, dose, or brain-training subscription is proven to prevent dementia ([WHO dementia risk-reduction guideline, second edition](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK623908/)). Treat this as optional quality of life, not another health quota.

### Stress and Recovery

Stress management is for reducing experienced stress and protecting behavior—not for claiming telomere repair. If stress is high, choose one practice that is acceptable enough to repeat: mindfulness, paced breathing, yoga, therapy skills, time in nature, journaling, or quiet leisure. Continue it only if it improves stress, sleep, mood, or behavior. Meta-analyses and randomized trials support small-to-moderate improvements in perceived stress in studied groups, but no universal practice or dose has proven superior ([PMID 38414520](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38414520/), [PMID 38862815](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38862815/)).

## Preventive and Environmental Maintenance

No lifestyle routine replaces prevention that must be matched to age, anatomy, history, exposures, and local guidance.

### Maintain a Preventive Plan

- Keep routine vaccinations current.
- Complete evidence-based screening when due; do not invent screening outside guidelines.
- Make sure blood pressure is screened. USPSTF recommends screening all adults and confirming an initially elevated result outside the clinic before treatment; routine self-checking is not required after a normal risk-appropriate screen ([USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/hypertension-in-adults-screening)).
- Review lipids and diabetes risk at the age- and risk-appropriate cadence rather than ordering broad recurring biomarker panels.
- Match sexual and reproductive prevention to the person's goals and exposures: contraception or pregnancy planning, barriers, and HIV/STI testing when applicable. Testing cadence depends on age, anatomy, sexual practices, partners, symptoms, pregnancy, and local risk ([CDC STI testing](https://www.cdc.gov/sti/testing/index.html), [CDC STI prevention](https://www.cdc.gov/sti/prevention/index.html)).
- Know major family history and share it with the clinician who sets the plan.
- Get dental prevention and treatment at a risk-appropriate interval.
- Include vision and hearing assessment when age, symptoms, work, or exposure make them relevant.
- Review prescriptions and supplements for continued need, interactions, and adverse effects.
- Follow through on abnormal findings. Elevated blood pressure, lipids, glycemic risk, or another screening result should lead to appropriate confirmation and evidence-based care—not indefinite repeat self-testing or wellness-score optimization.

CDC summarizes preventive care as routine medical and dental checkups, vaccinations, screening tests, and risk-informed counseling ([CDC preventive care](https://www.cdc.gov/chronic-disease/prevention/preventive-care.html)). Exact schedules change and should come from current CDC/USPSTF guidance and the treating clinician—not a static optimization checklist.

### Home, Air, and Food Safety

- **Radon:** test every home at least once if its status is unknown; it is the only way to know the level. Retest after major renovations or when occupying a different or lower level, then mitigate if the result meets the applicable action threshold ([EPA radon](https://www.epa.gov/radon), [EPA retesting](https://www.epa.gov/radon/how-often-should-i-testretest-my-home-radon)).
- **Wildfire smoke and particle pollution:** keep an exposure-triggered plan rather than buying a universal gadget. Check local air quality; during meaningful smoke, reduce infiltration and use a correctly sized portable HEPA cleaner or the highest compatible central-system filter, often MERV 13. Avoid ionizers and ozone-generating cleaners ([EPA indoor air filtration](https://www.epa.gov/wildfires/indoor-air-filtration)).
- **Food safety:** use the four routine steps—clean, separate, cook, and chill—with special care for raw animal products and perishable food ([CDC food safety](https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/prevention/index.html/)).

### Always-On Harm Reduction

- Do not use tobacco. If currently using it, evidence-based cessation support has far more value than adding wellness products. All forms of tobacco are harmful, and nicotine is addictive ([WHO tobacco and nicotine](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/tobacco)). Temporary nicotine-replacement treatment for quitting is different from starting nicotine as an optimizer.
- Minimize alcohol. Even low consumption carries some health risk, while most harm comes from heavier or episodic use. Zero is a valid and lowest-exposure choice ([WHO alcohol](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/alcohol)).
- Use seat belts, helmets for meaningful head-impact risk, hearing protection in loud environments, and functioning smoke/carbon-monoxide alarms. These are not glamorous or daily “stacks,” but preventing injury is part of maintaining a body.
- Use handwashing at ordinary key moments rather than compulsive over-washing.

## What Is Deliberately Out of the Core

Do not make routine stacks, broad testing, condition-specific drugs, recovery devices, or wearables part of the baseline unless they fill a demonstrated need or change an evidence-based decision. ## Cadence at a Glance

### Every Day

- Obtain at least seven hours of sleep and keep timing reasonably stable.
- Optionally use daytime light and early movement to support sleep timing.
- Move throughout the day; complete the planned training if scheduled.
- Eat mostly minimally processed foods; reach produce, fiber, and protein targets.
- Use clean, separate, cook, and chill practices when preparing or storing food.
- Drink according to thirst and conditions.
- Brush twice with fluoride toothpaste; clean between teeth once.
- Protect exposed skin from UV and use simple personal care.
- Use no tobacco; minimize alcohol.

### Every Week

- Reach the aerobic range and at least two strength sessions.
- Include some enjoyable movement, not only programmed exercise.
- Shop or prepare enough staple food to make the healthy choice easy.
- Protect recurring social time and genuine recovery time.
- Review adherence, energy, and training recovery; do not react to every daily fluctuation.

### Monthly or Quarterly

- Review training progression and whether the plan is still sustainable.
- If body composition is an active goal, assess multi-week weight/waist, fit, photos, and performance trends—not isolated readings.
- Replace a frayed toothbrush/brush head, usually around every three to four months.
- Stop optional products or practices that have produced no clear benefit.

### Once or When Triggered

- Test the home for radon if its status is unknown; retest after a major renovation or a meaningful change in occupied level.
- Activate the particle-pollution plan during wildfire smoke or poor-air episodes.
- Revisit sexual/reproductive prevention when goals, partners, practices, symptoms, pregnancy status, or exposure risk change.

### Clinician-Set or Annual Review

- Vaccinations and evidence-based screening.
- Medical, dental, vision, or hearing care at the appropriate risk-based interval.
- Medication/supplement reconciliation and family-history updates.
- Reassessment of persistent symptoms, injuries, fatigue, sleep problems, or major functional decline.

## A Low-Friction Rollout

Do not start twenty habits at once. A reasonable sequence is:

1. **Sleep and schedule:** protect sleep opportunity and stabilize timing.
2. **Movement:** establish two strength sessions and enough weekly aerobic time.
3. **Food environment:** make repeatable meals, protein, produce, and fiber easy.
4. **Harm reduction:** address tobacco, alcohol, and chronic sleep-disrupting caffeine.
5. **Maintenance:** lock in oral care, sunscreen, and simple grooming.
6. **Connection and prevention:** schedule relationships and close overdue preventive gaps.
7. **Optional layer:** test at most one goal-specific add-on at a time.

The sequence is an adherence tool, not an evidence ranking. If tobacco use, severe sleep loss, or an overdue high-value preventive service is present, that may move to the front.

## Minimal Scorecard

Track only measures that can change a decision:

| Measure | Useful review cadence | Decision it should inform |
|---|---:|---|
| Sleep opportunity and daytime sleepiness | Weekly trend | Earlier wind-down, caffeine timing, workload, or clinical assessment |
| Strength sessions and progression | Weekly/monthly | Exercise adherence and programming |
| Aerobic minutes or sessions | Weekly | Whether the public-health floor is being met |
| Energy and recovery, simple 1–5 rating | Weekly trend | Sleep, food, stress, training load, or symptom evaluation |
| Weight/waist/photos | Optional, every 2–4 weeks | Body-composition goal only |
| Preventive-care status | Annual/clinician-set | Book what is actually due |

Do not optimize the score instead of the person. Better function, stable energy, adequate sleep, stronger training, a healthy oral/skin baseline, and a routine that survives normal life are the outcomes.
